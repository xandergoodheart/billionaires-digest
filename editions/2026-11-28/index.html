Huang's Nvidia saga heads to Capitol Hill

Jensen Huang will give closed-door testimony to three House committees on Dec. 1, two weeks after his reinstatement as Nvidia's CEO, as lawmakers press for answers on chip-export compliance.

Huang set to face three House panels on Dec. 1

The move

Nvidia's Jensen Huang will give closed-door, transcribed interviews to the House Foreign Affairs, Intelligence and China Select committees on Dec. 1, with Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter following on Dec. 2, Al Jazeera reported Nov. 17. The committees requested the sessions after Huang resigned as CEO on Nov. 12 following reports of an affair with a woman U.S. authorities are investigating over suspected ties to Chinese intelligence; lawmakers say they want answers on Nvidia's role in Chinese access to its advanced chips. Nvidia's board reinstated Huang as CEO on Nov. 23.

Why it matters

It's the first time Huang himself, not just Nvidia's board or outside analysts, will face direct questioning from Congress on the spying allegations and chip-export compliance -- the answers could shape how aggressively Washington restricts Nvidia's China sales going forward.

The bear case

The sessions are closed-door transcribed interviews, not public hearings under oath, which gives Huang less immediate political exposure than a televised grilling, and lawmakers haven't said whether or when any transcript would be released.

What to watch

Whether lawmakers release the Dec. 1-2 transcripts, and whether any other committee calls Huang to a public hearing.

Tether's Devasini discloses 17.9% stake in Bitdeer

The move

Tether Global Investments Fund and Giancarlo Devasini reported beneficially owning 41,445,453 Class A shares of bitcoin miner Bitdeer Technologies Group -- 17.9% of the class -- in a Schedule 13D/A filed with the SEC on Nov. 27 for an event dated Nov. 25. It is the 14th amendment to a 13D first filed in 2024 and does not state whether the stake rose, fell, or simply moved between affiliated Tether entities.

Why it matters

Tether's investment arm has spent 2026 steadily building positions in bitcoin-mining and gold-adjacent companies alongside its core stablecoin business, making its SEC filings one of the most active disclosure streams among billionaires on this list.

The bear case

A 13D amendment can reflect an internal transfer between affiliated Tether entities rather than a new purchase, and this filing gives no transaction detail, so it isn't clear money changed hands on Nov. 25.

What to watch

Whether a future amendment or Bitdeer's own disclosures clarify the size and direction of any change in Tether's stake.

Tether's Devasini raises Versamet Royalties stake to 13.7%

The move

Tether Global Investments Fund, Tether International and Giancarlo Devasini disclosed owning 14,936,962 common shares of Versamet Royalties Corp. -- 13.7% of the class -- in a Schedule 13D/A filed Nov. 27 for an event dated Nov. 25. It's the second amendment to a 13D opened after Tether acquired roughly a 12.7% stake in Versamet from Royal Gold in a November 2025 deal and topped it up in February 2026.

Why it matters

The increase extends Tether's push into gold-royalty companies, a hedge-like bet that sits alongside its bitcoin-mining stakes and its core dollar-stablecoin business.

The bear case

A roughly one-point increase in a small-cap royalty company is a minor position size for an investor managing tens of billions in reserves, and the filing doesn't explain the strategic rationale.

What to watch

Whether Tether keeps adding to gold-royalty names or shifts focus back to its larger bitcoin-mining and crypto-adjacent stakes.

Airbnb bars hosts from demanding checkout chores

The move

Airbnb's updated house rules took effect Nov. 20, barring hosts from requiring guests to wash dishes, strip beds or take the trash outside at checkout, Skift reported. Hosts who repeatedly violate the policy can get a notice on their listing, lower search placement, or lose Superhost status; cleaning fees are still allowed.

Why it matters

Airbnb has faced years of guest complaints about long checkout chore lists, and standardizing what hosts can ask for is aimed at keeping Airbnb competitive with hotels, where guests do no cleanup at all.

The bear case

The policy still lets hosts charge cleaning fees and doesn't ban every chore request outright, so the practical change for many stays may be modest.

What to watch

Whether host pushback or workarounds, such as higher cleaning fees, prompt Airbnb to adjust the rule.
