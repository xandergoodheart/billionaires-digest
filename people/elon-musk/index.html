Goldman raises SpaceX price target to $230 before earnings

The move

Goldman Sachs raised its price target on SpaceX to $230 from $220 in an Oct. 7 note, keeping a Buy rating and implying about 45% upside from a $158.96 reference price. Goldman raised its SpaceX sales forecasts to roughly $51.6 billion for 2026, $127.5 billion for 2027 and $205.6 billion for 2028, and estimated AI-related revenue could reach $27.3 billion this year, rising to $146.7 billion by 2028. The note also cited Sensor Tower data showing Starlink's monthly active users up 21% year over year.

Why it matters

The upgrade reflects a Wall Street view that SpaceX's Starlink and AI-compute ambitions, not just rockets, are what will drive its valuation going forward.

The bear case

By the article's own math, SpaceX already trades at roughly 122 times this year's expected earnings at that reference price, a multiple that assumes years of fast, uninterrupted growth.

What to watch

SpaceX's upcoming earnings report, which Goldman's note was published ahead of.

Musk becomes a trillionaire again as SpaceX surges

The move

Elon Musk's net worth hit about $1 trillion on Oct. 5, up $30.6 billion from the prior session, as SpaceX shares rose more than 5% to around $167 and Tesla shares also gained after a strong delivery quarter. Morgan Stanley's Adam Jonas kept an overweight rating on SpaceX with a $300 price target. Musk also confirmed early talks with TSMC about a dedicated chip plant, Terafab, to supply Tesla, SpaceX and xAI. Musk briefly passed $1 trillion in June before giving it back as both stocks slid.

Why it matters

It's Musk's second trip over $1 trillion this year, and this time the move is tied to a strong Tesla delivery quarter, a clean Starship orbital launch and bullish analyst calls rather than hype alone.

The bear case

Both stocks are concentrated bets on Musk's own projects; if Starship or Tesla deliveries disappoint next quarter, the trillion-dollar mark could evaporate again as it did in June.

What to watch

Whether the SpaceX-TSMC Terafab talks turn into a signed deal.

Mark ZuckerbergPolicyAI & techMETA · TSLA · NVDAAI read · — Neutral

Zuckerberg, Musk, Huang sign voluntary AI safety accord

The move

Six AI and chip company leaders — Meta's Mark Zuckerberg, Google's Sundar Pichai, Nvidia's Jensen Huang, OpenAI's Greg Brockman, Anthropic's Dario Amodei and Tesla/SpaceX/xAI's Elon Musk — signed a one-page White House 'Accord on Super Intelligence' on Sept. 29. It commits signers to four layers of safety controls for frontier AI models: internal technical monitoring, an internal oversight team, an independent external auditor and an independent board committee. The accord is not legally binding and sets no penalties for falling short; it grew out of talks between Zuckerberg and House Speaker Mike Johnson, with Huang helping draft the principles beforehand.

Why it matters

It's a rare moment of several rival AI leaders agreeing to the same safety framework, even informally, while governments are still deciding how much to regulate AI.

The bear case

With no enforcement mechanism, public disclosure requirement or penalty for noncompliance, the accord is a voluntary statement of intent rather than a binding commitment.

What to watch

Whether any signer publishes audit results, or whether Congress tries to turn the accord into binding law.

Tesla's Cybercab certification deadline arrives under oath

The move

Tesla's deadline to answer a Sept. 10 NHTSA Special Order on its driverless Cybercab was Sept. 30. The order, part of Audit Query AQ26002 that NHTSA opened Sept. 3 after Tesla began commercial Cybercab service in Austin, demands 21 sworn answers, signed by a Tesla officer, on how the two-seat robotaxi, built with no steering wheel, pedals or mirrors, was certified as meeting federal motor vehicle safety standards written for human-driven cars. NHTSA says incomplete or false answers carry civil penalties of up to $139 million.

Why it matters

NHTSA escalated from informal questions to a legally binding order with sworn testimony, meaning a Tesla executive now carries personal liability for the completeness and accuracy of the answers.

The bear case

Tesla has shrugged off past regulatory probes before; unless NHTSA finds a clear violation in the sworn answers, this could end as one more audit that doesn't slow Cybercab's rollout.

What to watch

Whether NHTSA finds Tesla's Sept. 30 responses complete, or opens an enforcement case that could cap or ground the Cybercab fleet.

Tesla lines up $30 billion in credit for Cybercab, Semi, Optimus

The move

Tesla disclosed in a Sept. 29 SEC filing that it secured $30 billion in new credit facilities: a $20 billion line from Citibank and a $10 billion facility from Wells Fargo (an $8 billion revolver plus a $2 billion term loan). Tesla said the money is meant to help scale Cybercab production at Gigafactory Texas, the Semi truck line at its dedicated Nevada plant, and the Optimus humanoid robot, and that it does not plan to draw on the facilities in 2026.

Why it matters

Tesla is forecasting capital spending above $25 billion this year, up from about $8.5 billion last year, and wants financing lined up before it needs to spend rather than after.

The bear case

Lining up credit it says it won't use this year could also mean Tesla isn't yet confident its own cash flow covers the simultaneous ramp-up of three unproven product lines.

What to watch

Whether Tesla draws on either credit line in 2027, and Cybercab, Semi and Optimus production numbers in Tesla's next shareholder update.

SpaceX puts Starship into orbit for the first time

The move

SpaceX launched Starship's Flight 14 from Starbase, Texas, on Sept. 28. The rocket reached orbit for the first time and released 26 Starlink V3 satellites, then splashed down in the Northern Pacific after one of its six Raptor vacuum engines shut down early during ascent.

Why it matters

Reaching orbit is the milestone NASA's Artemis moon-landing plan and Starlink's global buildout both depend on, and it keeps SpaceX far ahead of any rival heavy-lift rocket.

The bear case

An engine cut out mid-ascent and the flight team cut the mission short as a precaution — Starship still has real reliability problems to work out before it can fly astronauts.

What to watch

The next Starship test flight and progress on the NASA-contracted human landing system.

SpaceX clears FAA hurdle for Starship's biggest test yet

The move

The FAA gave SpaceX final approval on Sept. 26 for Starship Flight 14, an orbital test that will deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites for the first time, using Ship 41 and Super Heavy Booster 21. The flight is set for Monday, Sept. 28, after a wet dress rehearsal and a delay tied to public-safety concerns.

Why it matters

A clean flight would prove Starship V3 can carry a full Starlink payload and reenter intact, a key step in SpaceX's push to keep launching at a pace that supports its roughly $1.95 trillion valuation.

The bear case

SpaceX already scrubbed and rescheduled this exact flight once this month; another delay or a failed reentry would raise fresh doubts about V3's reliability.

What to watch

Whether Flight 14 launches Monday and whether both stages survive reentry.

SpaceX stacks Starship for its first attempt to reach orbit

The move

SpaceX stacked its Ship 41 upper stage atop Super Heavy booster B21 at Starbase, Texas on Sept. 23, forming a 124-meter rocket for Starship's Flight 14, the vehicle's first attempt to reach orbit. The mission would deploy 26 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites over about a 10-hour flight, far longer than the roughly one-hour suborbital hops of earlier Starship flights. SpaceX is targeting a launch window opening as early as 7:15 a.m. CDT on Sept. 28, pending FAA approval; a temporary flight restriction is in place through Oct. 7.

Why it matters

An actual orbital attempt, rather than another suborbital hop, is the milestone SpaceX needs to start using Starship for real missions like Starlink deployment and, eventually, crewed flights.

The bear case

Earlier Starship test flights have ended in explosions or lost vehicles, and the FAA has not yet granted final approval for a vehicle this large to fly an orbital profile.

What to watch

The Flight 14 launch window opening Sept. 28, and whether the FAA grants approval in time.

US backs Musk's court fight against EU's $137M fine on X

The move

The US Justice Department filed Thursday to support Elon Musk and X's bid to have the European Commission's €120 million ($137 million) Digital Services Act fine thrown out at the EU's General Court. The Commission issued the fine in December 2025 over X's paid blue-checkmark verification, its ad database and limits on researcher access to public data.

Why it matters

It turns one company's regulatory fight into a US-versus-EU dispute over who gets to police American tech platforms operating in Europe.

The bear case

The EU said Friday it's ready to defend the fine vigorously in court, and Washington's support doesn't change the transparency violations the Commission cited.

What to watch

Whether the EU General Court sets a hearing date for the joint challenge from X and Musk.

Investors keep betting on Tesla's robotaxi story, not car sales

The move

Tesla shares rallied roughly 6.75% over the past week. Robotaxi service now runs in seven U.S. metros, and active FSD subscriptions have reached 1.48 million, up 56% year over year.
