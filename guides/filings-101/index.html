Filings 101

Most of what the world’s richest people do with public companies shows up first in a filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Here is what each common form is, when it’s filed, and what it can and can’t tell you, with real examples from the filings we track.

  1. Form 4
  2. Form 144
  3. 10b5-1 plans
  4. 13D vs 13G
  5. Form 13F
  6. 8-K
  7. S-1
  8. Form D

Form 4: insider trades

What it is
A report of a trade by a company insider: a director, an officer, or anyone who owns more than 10% of a company’s shares.
When it’s filed
Within 2 business days of the trade.
What it tells you
Who traded, which shares, how many, at what price, and how many they hold afterwards. A letter code says what kind of trade it was.
What it doesn’t
Why they traded. A sale is often routine, and big holders frequently sell on a pre-set schedule (see 10b5-1 plans below).
CodeMeaningHow to read it
POpen-market or private purchaseThey bought shares with their own money. The clearest signal.
SOpen-market or private saleThey sold. Often routine (taxes, diversification, a pre-set plan).
AGrant or award from the companyPay, not a purchase. Common for directors and executives.
MOption exercise or conversionTurned options or units into shares. Often paired with a sale.
GGiftShares given away, often to a family trust or charity. No money changes hands.
FShares withheld to pay tax or the exercise priceThe company kept some shares to cover a bill. Not a market sale.

Form 144: planned sales

What it is
A notice that an insider or other “affiliate” plans to sell restricted or control shares.
When it’s filed
On or before the day the sale order is placed, when the planned sales in a 3-month period are over 5,000 shares or $50,000.
What it tells you
That a sale is coming, roughly how many shares, and through which broker.
What it doesn’t
That the sale happened. The trade itself shows up later on a Form 4, and the planned amount can differ from what is actually sold.

10b5-1 plans: pre-set trading schedules

What it is
A written plan, set up in advance, that trades shares automatically on a schedule or at set prices. It lets insiders sell (or buy) even when they later learn private news.
When it’s filed
The plan itself is not filed with the SEC. Trades made under it are reported on Form 4, which has a box to mark trades made under a plan. Directors and officers must wait a cooling-off period before the first trade under a new plan.
What it tells you
That a trade was decided months earlier, so it says less about what the insider thinks today.
What it doesn’t
The plan’s full terms, or whether the plan was changed or cancelled before a trade.

Schedule 13D vs 13G: big stakes

What it is
Reports filed by anyone who owns more than 5% of a class of a public company’s voting shares. 13D is for owners who may try to influence or control the company (activists, acquirers). 13G is a shorter form for passive owners, like index funds or founders who are not seeking change.
When it’s filed
13D within 5 business days of crossing 5%, with amendments for material changes. 13G deadlines are longer and depend on the type of owner.
What it tells you
The size of the stake, and for a 13D the owner’s stated plans, such as pushing for board seats or a sale.
What it doesn’t
Day-to-day trading. Between amendments, the stake can change without a new filing.

Form 13F: quarterly fund holdings

What it is
A list of US-listed stocks and some other securities held by an investment manager that oversees $100 million or more in them.
When it’s filed
Every quarter, within 45 days after the quarter ends. So the snapshot is already up to 45 days old when it is filed.
What it tells you
What a fund (for example a billionaire’s family office or hedge fund) held at the end of the quarter, and by comparison with the last one, what it bought or sold.
What it doesn’t
Short positions, most non-US holdings, cash, or anything traded since the quarter ended. It is a snapshot, not a live portfolio.

8-K: company events

What it is
A company’s report of a major event: a merger, a CEO change, a big contract, results, a bankruptcy.
When it’s filed
Usually within 4 business days of the event.
What it tells you
That something important happened, in the company’s own words, often with the press release or agreement attached.
What it doesn’t
Analysis. The company decides how to describe the event.

S-1: going public

What it is
The registration statement a company files to sell shares to the public for the first time (an IPO).
When it’s filed
Before the IPO, often with several amendments (S-1/A) as the deal takes shape.
What it tells you
The company’s business, finances, risks, and who owns what before the listing, including founders’ and big investors’ stakes.
What it doesn’t
The final price or whether the IPO will happen. Those come later.

Form D: private raises

What it is
A short notice that a company or fund sold securities privately, without a public offering.
When it’s filed
Within 15 days after the first sale in the offering.
What it tells you
That a raise happened, roughly how much was offered and sold, and some of the people running the issuer.
What it doesn’t
Who invested or at what valuation.
